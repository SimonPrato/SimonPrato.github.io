Build your own line

Change the load resistance and reactance, the line’s characteristic impedance, or its electrical length. Presets reproduce the cases below. All examples assume an ideal, lossless line in sinusoidal steady state, with current defined from the input toward the load.

How to read the animations

The graphs show signed instantaneous voltage v and current i. Solid lines are the totals; dashed forward and dotted reflected waves travel in opposite directions. The pale envelopes show the peak magnitude at each position. Voltage is normalized to the incident peak voltage A; current to A/Z₀.

The shaded energy pockets below each graph are always positive: electric energy follows v², and magnetic energy follows i². Both use the same fixed scale. A large voltage pocket identifies strong electric-field storage; a large current pocket identifies strong magnetic-field storage.

The Smith chart is a phasor map, not a time plot. Its arc traces impedance from the load toward the input. “Scan line” moves the inspection point along that arc and marks the corresponding location on both wave graphs. The chart does not rotate just because time advances.

The equations behind every example

A line has distributed inductance L′ and capacitance C′ per unit length. Its characteristic impedance, speed and electrical length are

Z₀ = √(L′/C′),   vₚ = 1/√(L′C′),   θ = βℓ = 2πℓ/λ.

A prime means “per unit length.” Z₀ is the voltage-to-current ratio of one traveling wave; it is not a resistance that burns energy. For a load Zₗ = R + jX, the voltage reflection coefficient and input impedance are

Γₗ = Zₗ − Z₀Zₗ + Z₀,   Zᵢₙ = Z₀ Zₗ cos θ + jZ₀ sin θZ₀ cos θ + jZₗ sin θ.

The sine-and-cosine form also handles quarter-wave lines cleanly. On the Smith chart, moving a distance d from the load toward the source gives

Γ(d) = Γₗ e−j2βd,   Z(d) = Z₀ 1 + Γ(d)1 − Γ(d).

The magnitude of Γ stays constant on a lossless line. Its phase rotates clockwise: a one-way distance of λ/8 rotates the reflection coefficient by 90°, because reflection includes a round trip.

With x = 0 at the input and x = ℓ at the load, the simulator uses peak phasors

V(x) = A[ejβ(ℓ−x) + Γₗ e−jβ(ℓ−x)],
I(x) = AZ₀[ejβ(ℓ−x) − Γₗ e−jβ(ℓ−x)].

The plotted waves are Re{V(x)ejωt} and Re{I(x)ejωt}. The minus sign in the reflected current is essential: its wave travels backward, while our positive current direction stays toward the load.

Total reflection does not imply a 180° phase shift

An ideal reactive load absorbs no average power, so all incident power is reflected:

Γₗ = jX − Z₀jX + Z₀,   |Γₗ| = 1,   Pᵣₑꜰ / Pꜰₒᵣʷ = |Γₗ|² = 1.

This fixes the magnitude of the reflection coefficient, not its phase. For X = +Z₀, the reflected voltage shifts by +90°; for X = −Z₀, it shifts by −90°. A short circuit gives a 180° voltage reflection, while an open circuit gives a 0° voltage reflection. All four loads reflect all incident power.

Energy is a positive quantity, so it has no voltage-like “reflection phase.” Reflected power travels in the opposite direction. With a signed power-flow coordinate pointing toward the load, its average contribution is negative:

P̅ᵣₑꜰ,ₛᵢɡₙₑ𝒹 = −|Γₗ|² P̅ꜰₒᵣʷ.

That reversal of flow is distinct from a 180° oscillation phase shift. The reflected wave’s voltage and current differ by 180° under our fixed current-direction convention, regardless of whether the load is reactive or resistive. The incident and reflected currents at the input differ by 180° in the λ/8, Xₗ = Z₀ example because the load reflection phase and round-trip propagation phase combine there.

01 / Magnetic storage

Inductors: an open circuit at the input

An ideal inductor stores magnetic energy. Its impedance is Zₗ = jXₗ = jωL, so a lossless line terminated in it still presents a purely reactive impedance, except at poles where the input looks open. A lossless line cannot turn a purely reactive load into a dissipative resistor.

Zᵢₙ = jZ₀ Xₗ + Z₀ tan θZ₀ − Xₗ tan θ.

For ℓ = λ/8 and Xₗ = Z₀, tan θ = 1 and the denominator vanishes. The input is a voltage antinode and a current node: Zᵢₙ → ∞. Try pausing when the voltage is largest, then advance the cycle by 90° to see the energy move into the current pockets.

Why the reflection phases do not add up to 180° at the load

At this load, Γᵥ = j and Γᵢ = −j. These are the changes to the reflected waves. The inductor responds to the total voltage and current. If the incident load voltage is A, then

Vₗ = A(1 + j) = √2A ejπ/4,
Iₗ = (A/Z₀)(1 − j) = (√2A/Z₀)e−jπ/4.

Total voltage therefore leads total current by 90°, exactly as required by vₗ = L diₗ/dt. The reflected wave’s voltage and current differ by 180° because it travels backward. At the input, the −90° current reflection and −90° round-trip propagation phase give a 180° difference between incident and reflected currents. They cancel, while the voltages add.

Where the energy lives

uᴱ(x,t) = ½C′v²(x,t),   uᴹ(x,t) = ½L′i²(x,t),   Wₗ(t) = ½Liₗ²(t).

The line’s electric field balances the magnetic storage in both the line and the inductor. For this resonant standing wave, after choosing the time origin at maximum voltage,

Wᴱ(t) = W₀ cos²(ωt),
Wᴹ,ₗᵢₙₑ(t) + Wₗ(t) = W₀ sin²(ωt).

Their sum stays constant. Energy does not disappear; it alternates between the fields. This is parallel resonance as seen from the input. The animation depicts an established oscillation with stored energy. Starting it takes energy; real losses require power to maintain it. With no losses, the input accepts no average power.

A series winding resistance changes the result. At λ/8, a load R + jZ₀ gives Zᵢₙ = 2Z₀²/R − jZ₀. The input resistance becomes large when R is small, but finite; adding resistance also shifts the exact resonance.

02 / Electric storage

Capacitors: the complementary case

An ideal capacitor stores electric energy, with Zₗ = −jX꜀ and X꜀ = 1/(ωC) > 0. Current leads voltage by 90°. The transformed impedance is

Zᵢₙ = jZ₀ Z₀ tan θ − X꜀Z₀ + X꜀ tan θ.

For ℓ = λ/8 and X꜀ = Z₀, the numerator is zero: Zᵢₙ = 0. The input is a voltage node and a current antinode. The voltage reflection coefficient is −j; after the round trip it becomes −1 at the input, so voltages cancel and currents add.

i꜀ = C dv꜀/dt,   W꜀ = ½Cv꜀².

This is series resonance at the input. The line’s magnetic energy alternates with electric energy in the line and capacitor. A nonzero ideal voltage source connected directly across an ideal zero input impedance would demand unbounded current. These normalized plots instead specify a finite incident wave; a practical source has finite impedance and losses.

03 / Dissipation & transformation

Resistors: a different ratio, the same energy balance

A resistor turns absorbed energy into heat. A transmission line changes the voltage-to-current ratio seen by the source. It can increase the input resistance without creating energy or increasing the actual resistor value.

Zᵢₙ = Z₀ Rₗ + jZ₀ tan θZ₀ + jRₗ tan θ,   Zᵢₙ(λ/4) = Z₀²/Rₗ.

A 100 Ω quarter-wave line terminated in 50 Ω presents 200 Ω. At λ/8, the same load instead gives a complex input impedance:

Zᵢₙ(λ/8) = 2RₗZ₀²Rₗ² + Z₀² + j Z₀(Z₀² − Rₗ²)Rₗ² + Z₀² = 80 + j60 Ω.

Here |Γ| = 1/3, so 1/9 of the incident power is reflected and 8/9 is absorbed. The line’s fields still store energy, but net power flows into the resistor. For the simulator’s incident peak voltage A,

Pₗ = A²2Z₀(1 − |Γₗ|²) = ½Rₗ|Iₗ|².

The graphs use peak phasors, hence the factor ½. A lossless line satisfies Pᵢₙ = Pₗ; a real line also loses power in its conductors and dielectric.

Can inserting the line make the resistor burn out?

It can change power for a general source, but not through length alone in the specific case of a 100 Ω source resistance and a 100 Ω line. Let Vₛ be the source’s open-circuit RMS voltage. A direct 50 Ω load dissipates

Pₗ = Vₛ²Rₗ(Rₛ + Rₗ)² = Vₛ²/450.

With the λ/8 line, the total impedance is 180 + j60 Ω, so

Pₗ = Vₛ² Re(Zᵢₙ)|Rₛ + Zᵢₙ|² = 80Vₛ²180² + 60² = Vₛ²/450.

This equality holds for every length when Rₛ = Z₀. The matched source launches fixed incident power and absorbs the returning reflection. In general,

Pₗ = Vₛ²4Z₀(1 − |Γₗ|²)   when Rₛ = Z₀.

A 100 Ω source delivers its maximum available power to a 100 Ω input, not directly to 50 Ω. When Rₛ differs from Z₀, length-dependent interference can change the incident amplitude and load power. For example, Rₛ = 100 Ω, Rₗ = 50 Ω and Z₀ = √(100·50) ≈ 70.71 Ω make a quarter-wave line match the source. Load power rises from Vₛ²/450 to Vₛ²/400, a 12.5% increase. Whether a resistor fails depends on that power and its rating, cooling and duty cycle.

In a parallel RLC circuit, resonance similarly cancels the inductor and capacitor currents: ωC = 1/(ωL) and Zᵢₙ = R. The source supplies the resistor’s heat loss while energy oscillates between L and C.

04 / No load current

Open circuits: a voltage antinode

An open termination requires Iₗ = 0. Its voltage reflection coefficient is +1, and its current reflection coefficient is −1. Voltage doubles at the load, while the incident and reflected currents cancel.

Γₗ = +1,   Zᵢₙ = −jZ₀ cot θ.

At λ/8, the input is −jZ₀: a capacitive impedance. A quarter-wave open line looks like a short circuit; a half-wave line repeats the open termination.

No energy is dissipated in the open load. All incident power is reflected. Voltage antinodes have the largest electric-energy envelope, while current antinodes have the largest magnetic-energy envelope. Those antinodes are displaced by λ/4 when the line is long enough to contain both.

“Open” at the termination does not mean current is zero everywhere. Current along the line charges and discharges its distributed capacitance.

05 / No load voltage

Short circuits: a current antinode

A perfect short requires Vₗ = 0. Its voltage reflection coefficient is −1 and its current reflection coefficient is +1. Voltage cancels at the load; current doubles.

Γₗ = −1,   Zᵢₙ = jZ₀ tan θ.

At λ/8, the input is +jZ₀: an inductive impedance. A quarter-wave shorted line looks open, and a half-wave line repeats the short circuit.

The ideal short dissipates no power despite its finite current, because its voltage is zero. Energy remains in the line’s fields and travels back toward the source. A physical short has resistance and inductance, so it can dissipate power and depart from the ideal boundary condition.

Delay, phase and what the animation assumes

The termination does not change the propagation speed of the ideal line. A length λ/8 has a one-way delay T/8 and a round-trip delay T/4. The inductor or capacitor adds a frequency-dependent reflection phase; this can affect a reflected pulse’s timing and shape, without changing the line’s wave speed.

The simulator is a single-frequency steady-state model, not a switch-on transient. Changing electrical length changes ℓ/λ; changing reactance edits its value at that frequency. Absolute L, C, frequency and physical length are not inferred. The energy pockets show line storage only; load energy is described in the relevant section.

uᴱ / (½C′A²) = (v/A)²,   uᴹ / (½C′A²) = (Z₀i/A)².

Because L′ = Z₀²C′, both energy bands share a common normalization. Their full vertical scale is 4, the maximum squared wave amplitude for a passive load and a unit incident wave. Instantaneous voltage and current plots retain their signs; energy does not.
